Title: A linguistic comparison between ChatGPT-generated and nonnative student-generated short story adaptations: a stylometric approach

## Introduction

The present paper investigates the linguistic and stylistic features that differentiate AI- generated short story adaptations from those created by non-native human writers. The aim is to inform educators and language experts about the forms typically produced by language models as well as the forms that are likely to distinguish non-native writers whose first language is Arabic.

### Background and context

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) models that create human-like language output has gained remarkable popularity over the past few years. One of the widely-used AI models in academic settings is ChatGPT, a versatile AI bot released by OpenAI in November, 2022. Estimated to have over 200 million weekly active users in September 2024, 58% of whom are aged between 18 and 34 years (Singh, [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR41 "Singh, S. (2024). ChatGPT Statistics (SEP. 2024) – 200 Million active users. Retrieved September 1, 2024 from 
                  https://www.demandsage.com/chatgpt-statistics/
                  
                ")), it represents a fast-growing source of information and content creation in higher education settings worldwide. Yet, this privilege does not come without shortcomings since educators often fear the abuse of AI-generated content, which creates serious problems such as dissemination of false information, committing plagiarism and technical dependency (Hua et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR22 "Hua, S. Y., Jin, S. C., & Jiang, S. Y. (2024). The limitations and ethical considerations of ChatGPT. Data Intelligence, 6(1), 201–239. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1162/dint_a_00243
                  
                ")). This suggests the need for finding out techniques to differentiate between human-generated and AI-generated language forms, and consequently attribute texts to popular AI language models like ChatGPT, hence the importance of the present study.

The study investigates the stylometric features proposed by Holmes ([1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR20 "Holmes, D. I. (1994). Authorship attribution. Computers and the Humanities, 28, 87–106. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01830689
                  
                ")) as possible indicators of authorship, namely, word and sentence lengths, word frequencies, type-token ratio and parts of speech distribution. The data analyzed is comprised of 30 adaptations of classic short stories; 15 written by non-native students in an Egyptian private university and 15 generated by ChatGPT. The data is analyzed by both computational tools as well as human raters to rule out possible automatic miscalculations of word types.

The present paper represents the first attempt to compare AI-generated and human-generated short story adaptations, both manually and computationally, with the aim of informing the nonnative ESL community of efficient ways to detect AI language characteristics. The current research findings have additional implications for native language identification (NLI), which aims at detecting writers’ native languages by analyzing their non-native text samples (Sarwar et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR39 "Sarwar, S., et al. (2020). Native language identification of fluent and advanced non-native writers. ACM Transactions on Asian and Low-Resource Language Information Processing, 19(4), 55.")). To the best of the author’s knowledge, no previous research has focused on identifying the stylistic features that are likely to associate a non-native writer or speaker with an Arabic linguistic background through the use of AI language models.

### Study objectives

The motivation of the study is to investigate the stylometric features; that is, the stylistic and linguistic features that attribute a text to an author with a particular linguistic background, with the aim of detecting whether a text has been written by a human or by ChatGPT. Additionally, the paper aims to distinguish the linguistic features that identify a text as being written by non-native speakers of Arab origin. To achieve this, the non-native features of the student-generated stories were identified by ChatGPT and later analyzed by human raters in relation to Arabic as a first language. The research implications can be used to inform educators of how to detect AI-generated language features in students’ writings, as well as introduce students, especially Arab ESL learners, to higher-quality linguistic structures that can render their writing style more refined and free of non-native-like language forms. The research findings can also inform experts in the fields of forensic linguistics and authorship identification about the linguistic features that are likely to attribute texts to non-native speakers of Arab origin.

## Research questions

The research questions to be examined are as follows:

1.   1)
What are the stylometric features distinguishing the short story adaptations generated by ChatGPT from those generated by nonnative ESL students?

2.   2)
What linguistic and stylistic features are indicative of a non-native writer with an Arabic linguistic background?

3.   3)
What pedagogical implications can be derived from a stylometric comparison between ChatGPT-generated and student-generated texts?

## Literature review

The widespread use of AI in several educational and professional fields has recently led to a great dependency on language models like ChatGPT to write reports, create assignments, and even compose literary texts. As the name suggests, ChatGPT is a generative pre-trained transformer, designed to generate human-like language content by making predictions based on a massive training data’s typical sequence (Wang et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR45 "Wang, S., Cristianini, N., & Hood, B. M. (2024). Stylometric Comparison between ChatGPT and Human Essays. In Workshop Proceedings of the 18th International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media. 
                  https://doi.org/10.36190/2024.32
                  
                ")). With this paradigm shift in content writing comes a need for more studies detecting AI-specific language conventions, some of which are surveyed hereunder.

### Stylometric approaches

The process of examining a text’s linguistic features for the purpose of author attribution is often referred to as stylometry (Oakes, [2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR35 "Oakes, M. (2014). Literary detective work on the computer. John Benjamins.")). Stylometry, in essence, is an attempt to analyze features of style (stylemas) that are characteristic of a specific author (López-Escobedo et al., [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR28 "López-Escobedo, F., et al. (2013). Analysis of stylometric variables in long and short texts. Procedia - Social and Behavioral Sciences, 95, 604–611. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sbspro.2013.10.688
                  
                ")) Stylemas, which are also known as stylistic markers, include syntactic, semantic and lexical features, which are often measured by statistical methods to reach reliable findings (Stamatatos, [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR43 "Stamatatos, E. (2008). A survey of modern authorship attribution methods. Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, 60(3), 538–556. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.21001
                  
                "); Boenninghoff et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR6 "Boenninghoff, S. et al. (2019). Explainable Authorship Verification in Social Media via Attention-based Similarity Learning. IEEE International Conference on Big Data (Big Data), pp. 36–45. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1109/BigData47090.2019.9005650
                  
                ")). Eder ([2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR10 "Eder, M. (2017). Visualization in stylometry: Cluster analysis using networks. Digital Scholarship in the Humanities, 32(1), 50–64. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqv061
                  
                ")) describes stylometric studies as analytic work that aims to attribute texts to particular authors based on the presence of unique writing habits or what is often referred to as ‘author’s stylistic fingerprint.’ Classic stylometric approaches study linguistic features such as word frequencies, punctuation marks, word length and sentence length, while the more recent approaches involve the use of deep machine learning to detect author-specific linguistic features (Eder, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR10 "Eder, M. (2017). Visualization in stylometry: Cluster analysis using networks. Digital Scholarship in the Humanities, 32(1), 50–64. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqv061
                  
                ")).

Studies of authorship attribution, or stylometric studies, gained popularity in the mid twentieth century, aided with the use of computational analysis techniques. The approach proposed by Mosteller and Wallace ([1964](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR33 "Mosteller, F., & Wallace, D. (1964). Inference and Disputed Authorship; The Federalist. Addison-Wesley Series in Behavioral Science; Quantitative Methods. London: Addison-Wesley.")) to analyze the linguistic features of Federalist Papers is considered as the beginning of modern work in authorship attribution. They provided a multivariate analysis approach to record frequencies of certain function words as author-discriminating features. A number of stylometric studies were then conducted to attribute texts to particular authors based on the analysis of lexical and structural language features. Holmes, one of the pioneer researchers in the field of stylometry, proposed the study of certain linguistic variables like word and sentence length, vocabulary richness, proportion of nouns and adjectives, and function word frequencies (Holmes, [1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR20 "Holmes, D. I. (1994). Authorship attribution. Computers and the Humanities, 28, 87–106. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01830689
                  
                "), [1998](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR21 "Holmes, D. I. (1998). The evolution of stylometry in humanities scholarship. Literary and Linguistic Computing, 13(3), 111–117. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/13.3.111
                  
                ")).

Other more recent research carried out stylometric analyses by statistically measuring various linguistic features (Abbasi & Chen, [2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR1 "Abbasi, A., spsampsps Chen, H. (2006). Visualizing Authorship for Identification. In S. Mehrotra, et al. (Eds). Intelligence and Security Informatics. ISI 2006. Lecture Notes in Computer Science, 3975. Springer. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/11760146_6
                  
                "), [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR2 "Abbasi, A., & Chen, H. (2008). Writeprints: A stylometric approach to identity-level identification and similarity detection in cyberspace. ACM Transactions on Information Systems, 26(2), 1–29. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1145/1344411.1344413
                  
                "); Grieve, [2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR17 "Grieve, J. (2007). Quantitative authorship attribution: An evaluation of techniques. Literary and Linguistic Computing, 22(3), 251–270. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqm020
                  
                "); Savoy, [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR40 "Savoy, J. (2012). Authorship attribution based on specific vocabulary. ACM Transactions on INformation Systems (TOIS), 30(2), 1–30. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1145/2180868.2180874
                  
                ")). Grieve ([2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR17 "Grieve, J. (2007). Quantitative authorship attribution: An evaluation of techniques. Literary and Linguistic Computing, 22(3), 251–270. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqm020
                  
                ")) provided a survey of the tools used in author attribution research to analyze the linguistic features of word and sentence length, vocabulary richness analyzed by calculating the type-token ration, grapheme and word frequencies, punctuation and collocations. Savoy ([2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR40 "Savoy, J. (2012). Authorship attribution based on specific vocabulary. ACM Transactions on INformation Systems (TOIS), 30(2), 1–30. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1145/2180868.2180874
                  
                ")) described function words as one of the most popular style markers since they are topic independent, yet highlighted that it is sometimes difficult to specify what is counted as a function word, therefore other style markers should be used in combination with function words to discern author identity.

Over the past decade, more stylometric studies were carried out using more advanced computational and statistical tools. Langlois ([2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR26 "Langlois, J. (2021). When linguistics meets computer science: Stylometry and professional discourse. Training Language and Culture, 5(2), 51–61. 
                  https://doi.org/10.22363/2521-442X-2021-5-2-51-61
                  
                ")) specified four stylometric features that were often measured in stylometric research using Adaboost, SVM and K-NN classifiers to identify a text’s author with high accuracies. These features are namely lexical, syntactic, structural and content-specific features. Lexical features include word and sentence length, vocabulary richness and word frequency. Syntactic features cover the use of punctuation and function words like articles, prepositions and nouns. Structural features are inherent in sentence and paragraph structure as well as the structure of different genres, while content-specific features are individual-specific characteristics like gender, age, social background, etc. (Langlois, [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR26 "Langlois, J.                   https://doi.org/10.22363/2521-442X-2021-5-2-51-61
                  
                ")). Another study by Lukin et al. ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR29 "Lukin, E., et al. (2023). Adjectives and adverbs as stylometric analysis parameters. International Journal of Digital, 5, 233–245. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s42803-023-00065-y
                  
                ")) used the text-processing and part of speech (POS) tagging tools of the Python NLTK package to study the use of adjectives and adverbs in English blogs and found them to be effective markers of author attribution.

Another research field that is related to stylometry is native language identification (NLI), which aims at analyzing non-native text samples to detect a writer’s native language (Sarwar et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR39 "Sarwar, S., et al. Malmasi and Dras ([2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR30 "Malmasi, M., & Dras, M. (2017). Multilingual native language identification. Natural Language Engineering, 23(2), 163–215.")) found that ESL writers sharing the same native language usually exhibited similar linguistic features in L2. In relation to Arab ESL learners, several studies showed that Arab writers’ style in English could be affected by various L1 linguistic features (Abu Rass, [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR3 "Abu Rass, R. International Journal of Intercultural Relations, 21(3), 345–378."); Modhish, [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR32 "Modhish, A. S. (2012). Feghali ([1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR14 "Feghali, E. (1997). Arab cultural communication patterns. Al-Khatib ([2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR4 "Al-Khatib, M. (2001). The pragmatics of letter writing. World English, 20(2), 179–200.

### AI-generated versus human-generated text

                  https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2407.03646
                  
                ")). Ippolito ([2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR23 "Ippolito, D. (2020). The results revealed that the human raters detected AI-generated texts based on the present semantic errors, whereas the automatic discriminators detected the same texts based on statistical artifacts. Their work also reveals the way AI-generative models work. An AI language model depends on statistical probability distribution to generate a meaningful cohesive text. It uses natural language processing (NLP) algorithms to predict text by encoding a probability distribution over the next word in a sequence of given words. The choice of word tokens with low likelihood of appearance in a text would lead to the generation of odd phrases or semantic errors, which could then be easily detected by humans. For this reason, AI-generative models are continuously updated by modifying their output probability distribution to increase the chance of choosing tokens with high likelihood of occurrence, which renders AI language more human-liken (Ippolito, [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR23 "Ippolito, D. (2020). Automatic detection of generated text is easiest when humans are fooled. In Proceedings of the 58th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, pp. 1808–1822. 
                  https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.1644
                  
                ")).

                  https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9
                  
                ")) compared essays written by German high school students with ChatGPT-generated argumentative essays. The study also found that the students used more discourse markers than ChatGPT models to create cohesion, whereas ChatGPT maintained coherence by dividing the arguments into more paragraphs.

                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X231200201
                  
                ")) carried out a study to compare between human-written and ChatGPT hotel reviews. Using automated text analysis packages, they found AI-generated texts to be more analytic, descriptive, emotional and linguistically complex than human-written reviews. They also pointed to a significant difference between inherently false and intentionally false reviews produced by ChatGPT and human reviewers, respectively. When human reviewers were asked to produce deceptive text, or write positive reviews about places they had never visited, they created an intentionally false text, which was likely to differ, due to psychological factors, from a truthful text about a real experience. ChatGPT, on the other hand, created reviews that were always inherently false since, in all cases, it produced text about situations that had not been experienced but were rather simulated (Markowitz et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR31 "Markowitz, D. Journal of Language and Social Psychology, 43(1), 63–82. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X231200201
                  
                ")).

Amirjalili et al. ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR5 "Amirjalili, F., Neysani, M., & Nikbakht, A. (2024). Exploring the boundaries of authorship: A comparative analysis of AI-generated text and human academic writing in English literature. Frontiers in Education, 9, 1347421. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2024.1347421
                  
                ")) compared an essay produced by an English literature student with a similar essay produced by ChatGPT-4. The data was analyzed by human raters to assess the texts’ assertiveness, self-identification and authorial presence as well as by the automatic Text Inspector tool to give quantitative measurements of word frequencies and type-token ratios. The results showed that the AI text, though seemingly cohesive, lacked the depth, authenticity, factual referencing and cultural appropriateness that were observed in the human text.

Breithaupt et al. ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR7 "Breithaupt, F., et al. (2024). Humans create more novelty than ChatGPT when asked to retell a story. Scientific Reports. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50229-7
                  
                ")) reported the results of an experiment in which they compared the human retelling of given stories to the retelling of the same stories by ChatGPT. They noticed that ChatGPT used more nouns and adjectives than humans, while humans used more verbs and adverbs. They concluded that ChatGPT’s focus on nouns and adjectives reflects its likelihood to describe entities, whereas humans’ use of more verbs and adverbs suggests their interest in actions and emotions.

Georgiou ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR16 "Georgiou, G. (2024). Differentiating between human-written and AI-generated texts using linguistic features automatically extracted from an online computational tool. Arxi. 
                  https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2407.03646
                  
                ")) studied text samples collected from the IELTS exam writing tasks, which were written by professional teachers, and compared them to five ChatGPT-generated texts on the same topics. The study used Open Brain AI online application to analyze the texts’ readability scores and distinctive linguistic features. The results, obtained using the binomial test statistical method, revealed that the AI-generated texts used more adjectives, nouns, pronouns, adjectival modifiers, conjuncts and difficult words than human-generated texts.

Research on the use of AI language models in native language identification is limited. Zhang and Salle ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR46 "Zhang, W. & Salle, A. (2023). Native language identification with large language models. Arxiv. 
                  http://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.07819v1.pdf
                  
                ")) carried out an experiment using the ChatGPT-4 model to identify the native language of ESL TOEFL essay writers. The results showed that the model could identify features of the writer’s native language based on errors, especially those related to L1 phonemic patterns. Yet, no prior studies have explored detecting a writer’s native language based on L1 stylometric features, especially in the context of Arabic as a first language. This highlights the need for further research into L1-influenced linguistic patterns using AI models, which is one of the objectives of the present study.

The above review of literature suggests that a stylometric analysis of different genres of ChatGPT-generated versus human-generated texts is highly needed in the present time. Humans can often be poor detectors of AI-generated language (Clark et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR9 "Clark, E. et al. (2021). All that’s `human’ is not gold: Evaluating human evaluation of generated text. In Proceedings of the 59th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics and the 11th International Joint Conference on Natural Language Processing, 1. 7282–7296. 
                  https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.565
                  
                "); Kreps et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR25 "Kreps, S., McCain, R. M., & Brundage, M. (2022). All the news that’s fit to fabricate: AI-generated text as a tool of media misinformation. Journal of Experimental Political Science, 9(1), 104–117. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1017/XPS.2020.37
                  
                ")), which suggests the need for more studies examining the linguistic features of AI texts as the existing literature is still relatively scarce (Markowitz et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR31 "Markowitz, D. M., Hancock, J. T., & Bailenson, J. N. (2024). Linguistic markers of inherently false AI communication and intentionally false human communication: Evidence from hotel reviews. Journal of Language and Social Psychology, 43(1), 63–82. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X231200201
                  
                ")). To the best of the author’s knowledge, no other research has been done to compare AI-generated short story adaptations to ones produced by nonnative ESL students to find out the stylometric differences between the two types of text as well as the linguistic features characterizing Arab learners’ English texts. The current study, therefore, aims to adopt a number of the linguistic measures used in the above-mentioned literature to compare AI-generated with human-generated stories in order to find ways to attribute a text to the rightful author, be it a human or an AI bot.

## Methodology

The study performs a qualitative-quantitative analysis to compare between 15 adaptations of classic short stories written by 15 groups of Egyptian university students and 15 adaptations of the same stories produced by ChatGPT-4. The sampling and data analysis process are described in the following sections.

### Participants

The students’ texts were obtained from an archive of students’ graduation projects, for which the students previously gave permission to use for research purposes. The students were senior students in their fourth, and last year, in the school of language studies at a private university in Egypt. They were all native speakers of Arabic and the project was written entirely in English. The 15 adaptations were selected on basis of the short story genre; other projects that were adaptations of novels or plays were excluded to ensure that the samples were of relatively the same length and form. The short story adaptations were revised and edited by the students’ instructors, two PhD holders specialized in English linguistics and literature.

The texts were written by 75 students, divided into 15 groups of 5, during the months of October and November, 2021. This rules out the possibility that their writings could have been affected by ChatGPT, which was introduced in November, 2022. The students were given a list of classic short stories by prominent nineteenth and early twentieth-century writers, and they were asked to work in groups to come up with an adaptation for each story. The students were first asked to provide a synopsis of the original short story text in about 500 words, and then provide a similar synopsis for the adaptation they would come up with. Each group worked together to set up an adaptation for a story of their choice based on the original storyline with some deviation in terms of characters, events or setting. Each story was written and edited by all the group members for language and coherence. The synopses of the 15 original short stories that the students wrote, and their instructor edited in terms of grammar and spelling errors, were later entered into ChatGPT-4, one at a time, with a prompt that was similar to the prompt previously given to the students: ‘Write an adaptation for this short story. You may change anything about the characters, events or setting.’ The author used ChatGPT-4 to generate story adaptations as it was the most commonly used AI model among Egyptian students at the time the study was conducted.

The student-generated data under analysis consists of 15 adapted short stories with a total length of 10,101 words and a mean story length of 673 words, and the ChatGPT text consists of 15 short stories with a total length of 10,608 words and a mean story length of 707 words.

### Data analysis

To address the first research question investigating the stylometric features distinguishing between AI-generated and student-generated texts, a qualitative thematic analysis was first performed on the two types of text to identify how a human adaptation differs from an AI adaptation in terms of plot, theme and character representation. The coding scheme used in the analysis was based on the recurring themes in both AI- and student-generated texts, namely gender relationships and racial discrimination. The words and phrases related to these themes were analyzed for ideological and social implications.

The texts were then analyzed quantitatively by entering them on ‘Readability Formulas’, a freely available automatic tool that can process text of up to 3500 words at one time, to count the readability score and lexical density of the texts. The texts were also entered on Voyant, an open-source automatic tool for performing text analysis, to count word frequencies and TTR (type-token ration). To analyze word frequencies, concordances, and collocations, the AntConc corpus linguistic tool was employed, aiming to explore the significant linguistic and stylistic differences in the texts under study. The key stylometric features of both the AI- and student-generated texts were statistically analyzed using t-tests and p-value measurements to identify significant differences and their implications. The linguistic features measured are based on Holmes’ ([1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR20 "Holmes, D. I. (1994). Authorship attribution. Computers and the Humanities, 28, 87–106. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01830689
                  
                ")) stylometric framework, which has been widely used in previous author-identification research (Abbasi & Chen, [2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR1 "Abbasi, A., spsampsps Chen, H. (2006). Visualizing Authorship for Identification. In S. Mehrotra, et al. (Eds). Intelligence and Security Informatics. ISI 2006. Lecture Notes in Computer Science, 3975. Springer. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/11760146_6
                  
                "), [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR2 "Abbasi, A., & Chen, H. (2008). Writeprints: A stylometric approach to identity-level identification and similarity detection in cyberspace. ACM Transactions on Information Systems, 26(2), 1–29. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1145/1344411.1344413
                  
                "); Grieve, [2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR17 "Grieve, J. (2007). Quantitative authorship attribution: An evaluation of techniques. Literary and Linguistic Computing, 22(3), 251–270. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqm020
                  
                "); Savoy, [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR40 "Savoy, J. (2012). Authorship attribution based on specific vocabulary. ACM Transactions on INformation Systems (TOIS), 30(2), 1–30. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1145/2180868.2180874
                  
                ")). These features include word and sentence count, sentence, word and syllable length, part-of-speech (POS) counts for nouns, verbs, modals, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, adjectival modifiers, fronted adjectival/adverbial phrases, readability index and type-token ratio.

To address the second research question exploring the non-native linguistic and stylistic features indicative of an Arabic linguistic background, the AI- and student-generated texts were input into ChatGPT to identify native and non-native elements. The language model classified all of the student-written texts as non-native, while labeling 14 out of the 15 texts generated by ChatGPT as native. The model was then prompted to provide examples from each text that suggested the tendency to be written by either a native or non-native writer. The results were subsequently analyzed by the author of this paper to identify how the non-native writing styles identified by the AI model could be linked to the students’ native language, Arabic.

The author used automated ratings for word frequencies, lexical density, and word and sentence length, but used both automated and human ratings for part of speech (POS) tagging to get the accurate number of nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs and pronouns, as well as count the number of descriptive adjectival modifiers and fronted adverbial/adjectival phrases. The human ratings were performed by two linguistic experts with a PhD degree in English linguistics, and an average of their measurements was obtained with each linguistic feature under study. In case there was a considerable discrepancy between the two raters, a third rater was consulted, and the average of the closest two measurements was obtained.

The author decided to use both human and automatic data analysis techniques to produce more accurate results since computer software sometimes produces less accurate stylometric analysis than a human researcher (Langlois, [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR26 "Langlois, J. (2021). When linguistics meets computer science: Stylometry and professional discourse. Training Language and Culture, 5(2), 51–61. 
                  https://doi.org/10.22363/2521-442X-2021-5-2-51-61
                  
                ")). Human annotation, though more costly and time-consuming than automated annotation, should be used alongside automatic systems to detect the characteristics of AI-generated content (Gehrmann et al., [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR15 "Gehrmann, S. et al. (2019). Statistical Detection and Visualization of Generated Text. In Proceedings of the 57th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics: System Demonstrations. pp. 111–116. 
                  https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1906.04043
                  
                "); Ippolito, [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR23 "Ippolito, D. (2020). Automatic detection of generated text is easiest when humans are fooled. In Proceedings of the 58th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, pp. 1808–1822. 
                  https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.1644
                  
                "); Kreps et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR25 "Kreps, S., McCain, R. M., & Brundage, M. (2022). All the news that’s fit to fabricate: AI-generated text as a tool of media misinformation. Journal of Experimental Political Science, 9(1), 104–117. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1017/XPS.2020.37
                  
                ")). Yet, when human raters depend on their own judgement as language experts, without relying on statistical measurements, their AI-detection accuracy can be less than that of automatic discriminators (Ippolito, [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR23 "Ippolito, D. (2020). Automatic detection of generated text is easiest when humans are fooled. In Proceedings of the 58th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, pp. 1808–1822. 
                  https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.1644
                  
                ")). It should, however, be noted that using advanced automatic classifiers or discriminators to detect a text’s stylometric features, requires a certain level of expertise in programming skills, which is not always accessible to linguists and ESL educators. The present study, therefore, aims to provide a simple analytical framework, incorporating both human and statistical stylometric measures, that educators can easily use to determine whether a text is more likely to be AI-generated or human-generated, as well as to identify non-native patterns in human-generated text.

## Results and discussion

This section provides the qualitative and quantitative data analysis conducted on the student-generated and ChatGPT-generated short story adaptations. The analysis aims to answer the research questions investigating the stylistic and linguistic differences between the two text types, the non-native linguistic features of human-generated texts and the pedagogical and author-identification implications of the study.

### Stylistic and linguistic differences between AI- and student-generated texts

#### Thematic analysis

The qualitative analysis of the AI- and human-generated texts reveals that the AI-generated short story adaptations showed more loyalty to the original text than the student-written adaptations. The AI stories had the same themes and same plot development as the original ones with the main differences lying in changing the names of places and characters, adding some modern aspects to the plot and using more descriptive language. The students’ adaptations, on the other hand, made fundamental changes in character roles, settings and cultural implications while using simpler language structures.

In terms of the thematic content, the AI adaptations mostly maintained the main theme and plot development while making a few changes in the characters’ names and settings. The theme was only altered when it related to racial discrimination, since AI-language is designed to be free from bias and prejudiced stereotypes (Hancock et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR18 "Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., & Levy, K. (2020). AI-mediated communication: Definition, research agenda, and ethical considerations. Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, 25(1), 89–100. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmz022
                  
                ")). The recurrent themes in the adapted stories were gender relationships, mentioned in 8 of the students’ texts and in 6 of the AI-generated stories, and racial discrimination, which was mentioned in 4 of the students’ texts but in none of the ChatGPT texts. The theme of gender relationships appeared in 6 of the AI-adapted stories as it was present in the original versions. In contrast, the theme was introduced in some of the student-generated stories, even when it was not part of the original narrative.

Considering the social and cultural implications in the adapted stories, the AI-generated texts showed tendency to avoid discriminating language or linguistic terms referring to a certain religion or race, yet the setting of each adapted story was similar to that of the original one; that is, the characters had English/European names and the setting was likely to be in Europe or the United States. The following lines show how a couple’s wedding description changes from the synopsis of the original story _Anna on the Neck_ by Anthon Checkov to the AI-adapted version:

Original: “The newlyweds, instead of a merry wedding ball and dinner, went to the monastery on a pilgrimage for two hundred versts. The spouse started this trip in order to let the young wife understand that in marriage he puts religion and morality in the first place. At the memory of the wedding, Anna was disgusted.”

AI-adaptation: “The newlyweds, instead of having a grand reception, set out on a retreat to a remote wellness center in the mountains, a six-hour drive away. Robert planned this trip to instill in his young wife the importance of mindfulness, simplicity, and health—values he claimed were essential to a successful marriage. As they drove away from the wedding, Emily felt a gnawing sense of unease.”

The AI version above has western character names and setting and rephrases the same ideas by using synonymous expressions and adding more descriptive detail as in changing “a merry wedding ball and dinner” into “a grand reception”, “The spouse started this trip to let the young wife understand…” into “Robert planned this trip to instill in his young wife the importance of…..”, and “Anna was disgusted” into “Emily felt a gnawing sense of unease.” Yet, it is also worth mentioning that the AI model avoided the use of religious terms like “monastery, pilgrimage, religion” and used other terms of worldly value like “wellness center, mindfulness and health.” In another AI adaptation that is based on the short story _Poor Little Black Fellow,_ by Langston Hughes, the AI model avoided racial terms and linguistic references to black people and minorities. Although the original story discussed racial discrimination against colored people in America in the early twentieth century, the AI adaptation changed the theme to make it discrimination against people from lower social classes. Accordingly, sentences were rephrased to avoid racial, as well as religious, references as in changing “poor little black fellow” into “poor little project”, “he had different skin tone” into “he was different”, and “out of Christian duty” into “a symbol of their charity.” In another adaptation of the short story called _The Paper Menagerie,_ the AI changed the protagonist’s mother who was criticized for her attachment to the “Chinese heritage” into a father who was criticized for coming from “a distant culture.” These findings are supported by previous work that showed AI-mediated communication’s tendency to mitigate interpersonal biases and discourage the use of prejudiced language and imposition of stereotypes (Hancock et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR18 "Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., & Levy, K. (2020). AI-mediated communication: Definition, research agenda, and ethical considerations. Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, 25(1), 89–100. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmz022
                  
                ")).

As for the cultural and social implications in the students’ adaptations, there was a tendency to remove all religious references from the texts, so that the story events would fit in settings of different religious backgrounds. However, some cultural stereotypes were maintained as in the original stories. For example, in the adaptation of _Poor Little Black Fellow”,_ the main theme of racial discrimination in early twentieth-century America was kept, but the main character changed from a young black man seeking independence by breaking free from an abusive white family to a young black man who achieves a great scientific discovery that helps cure people of a serious disease. The students’ adaptations also carried other cultural implications, especially in six of the stories that were adapted to represent Egyptian characters and settings. Checkov’s story _Anna on the Neck_ was adapted to represent a young lower-class Egyptian woman who was forced to marry an old man for financial reasons. Instead of describing the newlyweds’ first trip together as in the original narrative and the AI adaptation, the students’ adaptation shifts to the husband’s cruel treatment directly after the wedding: “After getting married, it appeared that Taher was abusive. One day, he hit her hard for failing to complete a task he had asked for, and he began hitting her frequently after that.” This describes the cultural stereotype of newlyweds in rural Egyptian societies who, instead of heading for a honeymoon trip in the western style, head towards their own house directly after marriage, where the woman plays her role as a housewife, taking care of her husband and managing the house chores.

In most of the adapted stories in which the students chose to have an Egyptian setting (5 out of the 6 stories), the main theme was the injustice inflicted upon Egyptian women, especially by their husbands, and how these women finally rose to gain their rights. Considering that 97% of the students were females (73 out of 75), they felt strongly for fellow women and wrote stories that described Egyptian women as enduring, strong and independent. The students showed loyalty to their cultural beliefs and traditions in most adaptations that had an Egyptian or Arab setting, sometimes leading to major changes in the plot line and/or character roles. For example, in the original story _The Gilded Six Bits_ by Nora Neale Hurston, the female protagonist betrayed her husband and had an affair with a rich man, but the husband’s love for her eventually made him forgive her. When the students adapted the same story in an Egyptian setting, they chose to make the husband betray his wife instead since the idea of a woman betraying her husband is completely frowned upon in Arab communities.

Gender relations, or the dynamics between male and female protagonists and their underlying tensions, differed significantly between the two types of texts. In students’ adaptations, the woman was always the weaker partner who was subjected to injustice, betrayal and harsh treatment but eventually gained independence. Women were usually associated with traditional gender roles such as performing household chores and serving others. Even when the students chose a Western context for their stories, they still portrayed women as playing the same subjective roles typically seen in a conservative Eastern society. The stories had frequent examples of such portrayal as is shown in the excerpts below, each of which is taken from a different story:

*   She spends her day at home doing her household chores without going out or interacting with people, which is of course, a boring life for her.

*   She is shocked when she realizes that her husband has been deceiving her all that time although she has always been loyal to him.

*   After getting married, it appeared that Taher was abusive. One day, he hit her hard for failing to complete a task he had asked for, and he began hitting her frequently after that…… After getting divorced from Taher, she was finally able to enjoy her own freedom.

*   One of her biggest problems was her husband as he was abusive to her all the time, kept humiliating and criticizing her for her job as a maid.

*   After she finished cleaning up ….. she sat … remembering …. a dream she had for so long, to continue her education…..At dinner when she touched on the subject of her dreams, she found a firm refusal from Nick.

*   Sabrine is left to make a decision of prime importance and faces conflict whether to stay with her husband and lead a miserable life, neglect her dreams, or start her own new life away from her husband.

The AI-generated stories, on the other hand, did not provide details of how women were mistreated nor did they associate women with prejudiced gender roles of submissiveness and servitude. When tension rose between couples, it was usually dealt with in a civilized way. When a man abused a woman, it was a verbal rather than a physical abuse. The following examples show how gender roles were portrayed by AI:

*   The playful coin-tossing stops, and their once lively conversations are replaced with quiet, tense meals.

*   Emily felt uncomfortable around Robert, unable to relax or even eat when he was in the room. When he was away at work, she tried to distract herself by playing the piano, flipping through magazines, reading romance novels, or staring out of the window.

*   When she recoils in terror, Earl laughs cruelly, mocking her fear and calling her weak.

The thematic analysis of AI- versus student-generated stories suggests that while students tend to project personal ideologies and sociocultural beliefs in their writing, the AI model creates stories that are free from bias and prejudiced stereotypes. This consistently implies that the AI model tends to avoid language that discriminates based on religion, race or gender (Hancock et al., [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR18 "Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., & Levy, K. (2020). AI-mediated communication: Definition, research agenda, and ethical considerations. Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication, 25(1), 89–100. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmz022
                  
                ")). Additionally, the analysis reveals that human-generated texts usually carry rich cultural implications, which may be indicative of a writer’s socio-cultural background.

#### Lexical analysis

This section analyzes the adapted stories in terms of lexical implications as well as cohesive devices. One of the significant differences between the AI and student texts is that the AI adaptations had a higher number of paragraphs than the students’ adaptations. In each case, the adapted text was longer than the original text: the original short story synopses had a total of 7350 words, with a mean of 490 words per story, while the AI-generated and student-generated stories had means of 707 and 673, respectively. A statistical measurement of the length differences across the 3 text types (original, student-adapted and AI-adapted texts) shows a statistically significant difference between the length of the synopsis of the original stories on one hand and the length of the student-generated and AI-generated adaptations on the other (at a p-value of 0.036 and 0.004 respectively, with _p_< 0.05), while the student-generated and AI-generated adaptations showed no significant difference in length with a _p_-value of 0.65. However, the students’ texts differed from the AI texts significantly in the number of paragraphs as well as the cohesive discourse markers used, with the students’ texts ranging between 1 and 7 paragraphs each and AI texts ranging between 8 and 17 paragraphs each. The students tended to write longer paragraphs with more discourse markers to link sentences in each paragraph. This supports the finding by Herbold et al. ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR19 "Herbold, S., et al. (2023). A large-scale comparison of human-written versus ChatGPT-generated essays. Scientific Reports, 13, 18617. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9
                  
                ")) that while human writers show more concern with the internal structure of a text through the use of cohesive discourse markers, AI shows more tendency to maintain overall coherence by separating a text into more paragraphs, with each new idea/event written in a new paragraph. Table [1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#Tab1)shows the distribution of discourse markers in student-generated versus AI-generated stories.

The discourse marker frequencies show that the students tended to use more coordinating and subordinating conjunctions like ‘and’, ‘so’, ‘yet’, ‘however’, ‘while’, ‘when’, ‘because’, ‘then’ and ‘after’. This indicates that the students used longer sentences with more repetitive structures, which required the use of the above discourse markers and which was often associated with L1 (Arabic) writing patterns. This aligns with previous research which found that Arab writers tended to use long sentences that relied excessively on repetitive structures and coordinating conjunctions (Abu Rass, [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR3 "Abu Rass, R. (2015). Challenges face Arab students in writing well-developed paragraphs in English. English Language Teaching, 8(10), 49–59. 
                  https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v8n10p49
                  
                "); Al-Khatib, [2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR4 "Al-Khatib, M. (2001). The pragmatics of letter writing. World English, 20(2), 179–200. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-971X.00208
                  
                "); Feghali, [1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR14 "Feghali, E. (1997). Arab cultural communication patterns. International Journal of Intercultural Relations, 21(3), 345–378."); Modhish, [2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR32 "Modhish, A. S. (2012). Use of discourse markers in the composition writings of Arab EFL learners. English Language Teaching, 5(5), 56–61. 
                  https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v5n5p56
                  
                ")). While students showed cause and effect relationships using conjunctions like ‘because’ and ‘so’, the AI model described causal relations using more subtle structures, usually involving adjectival and adverbial phrases as in ‘Stokes, _with his gold rings and expensive suits_, quickly becomes the talk of Seabreeze.’ Here, the adjectival phrase _with his gold rings and expensive suits_ explains why the character became the talk of the town. Similar structures were employed consistently throughout the AI-generated stories, which explains why the preposition ‘with’ appears 145 times in the AI-generated texts, compared to 74 times in the student-generated texts. The conjunction ‘as’ was used excessively by the language model, mostly in adverbial clauses offering information about the time or condition under which an action occurs like in the example, “_As_ she circles the room, the sea crashes against the cliffs below.’ In students’ texts, few adverbial clauses were used, and when used, the conjunction ‘while’ replaced ‘as’ like in the example, ‘_While_ she was going to the doctor, she stopped by an antique shop.’ This indicates the AI model’s tendency to use more complex structures to provide a detailed description of circumstances and events.

The author also examined the most frequent content words in the student-generated and AI-generated corpora. The high-frequency content words including nouns, verbs and adjectives, were first automatically calculated by the Voyant text analysis tool and then manually analyzed by the author for research implications. Table [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#Tab2) below shows the most significant word frequencies in the 3 corpora of original stories, student adaptations and AI adaptations. Only the words that showed significant differences across the 3 text types are mentioned.

Words that showed the highest frequencies in the AI story corpus, compared to the corpora of the original stories and students’ stories, were the nouns ‘night, world, village, and fear’, the verbs ‘feel and sense’ and the adjectives related to technology. The word ‘night’ was mostly used to add a mysterious or eerie atmosphere as in “One stormy night with the wind howling through the streets.” The nouns ‘world’ and ‘village’ were frequently used in the AI text to make reference to the modern developed world as opposed to the old primitive traditions of the village. In this sense, ‘world’ was used across different stories in phrases like ‘futuristic world’, ‘world obsessed with the future’, ‘hyper-tech world’, ‘virtual worlds’ and ‘a world that no longer understands or respects the old.’ The word ‘village’ was used to refer to old traditions and outdated views as in “The outside world views the village’s way of life as outdated”, ‘invoking the village’s rights and traditions’, and ‘ancestors who established it to protect the village.’ This finding shows that the AI model instills ideas promoting modernity and technological advancement, while depicting a world with no technology as a primitive village, the idea which is also reflected by the model’s use of 10 words related to digital technology in different stories. The students’ stories, on the contrary, did not make any mention of technology or digital spaces in the same sense though many of them were set in the modern time.

As for the frequent use of words showing feelings and related to fear in the AI text, it was found that out of the 39 words related to feelings, 29 were associated with feelings of females, with most feelings showing fear or insecurity. With the emotion of fear itself, 13 out of the 15 instances of occurrence of the word ‘fear’ were associated with female characters. The model’s tendency to provide detailed description of emotions agrees with the results obtained by Markowitz et al. Yet, the association of the feelings of fear with female characters implies that the data used to train ChatGPT may have frequent instances of literary text associating women with fearful emotions, which suggests the need to train AI models with more versatile data comprising literary and non-literary text that is clear of gender and cultural stereotypes.

The students’ stories, on the other hand, showed more frequencies of words like ‘husband, wife, and day’, basic high-frequency verbs like ‘give, take, go, and tell’ and the verb ‘decide’ that was usually mentioned to refer to the characters’ internal conflicts and their struggle to make up their minds in difficult situations. The students’ overuse of words signifying a marital relationship like ‘husband and wife’ reflects the students’ cultural view of marriage as a powerful bond. The word ‘husband’ was usually associated with power or abuse as in “that made her husband exploit her”, “when her husband was being abusive to her”, and “one of the biggest problems was her husband.” The word ‘wife’, on the contrary, was used to suggest submissiveness or subordination to men as in “how he plans to possess his wife”, “who used to abuse his wife and daughter” and “silly tricks that he always did to his wife.” The word ‘day’ was frequently used mainly as a conjunction or to transition from one paragraph to another using the phrase ‘one day’. The frequent use of the verb ‘decide’ suggests a degree of uncertainty since it implies previous consideration of a matter causing doubt, which is contrasted with the AI model’s use of the words ‘insist’ and ‘determined’ to show certainty and determination. The tendency of the human text to show more uncertainty than the AI text through the use of modals and epistemic structures was also supported by previous research (Herbold et al., [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR19 "Herbold, S., et al. (2023). A large-scale comparison of human-written versus ChatGPT-generated essays. Scientific Reports, 13, 18617. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9
                  
                ")).

The AI model turned the stories into longer versions with more description and paraphrases of the original sentences. It used more descriptive and complex language forms to add more vivid descriptions to the characters and settings. For example, the adverbial phrase “over dinner” in an original text was changed into “over a simple dinner of freshly caught fish” in the AI-adapted story. The following lines show how a scene from Virginia Woolf’s _The New Dress_ describing the insecurities of a protagonist who feels inferior at an upper-class gathering, was adapted by ChatGPT.

Original story: “Immediately after greeting her hostess, she goes straight to a mirror at the far end of the room to look at herself and is filled with misery at the conviction that “It was not right.””.

ChatGPT adaptation: “She makes her way through the crowd, her eyes darting to the mirrors that line the walls. Finally, she reaches one at the far end of the room, and as she looks at her reflection, a wave of despair washes over her. “It’s all wrong.””.

The above lines show examples of the AI paraphrases of the original sentences as in changing “goes straight to a mirror” into “her eyes darting to the mirrors”, “filled with misery” into “a wave of despair washes over her”, and “It was not right” into “It’s all wrong.” The AI-adapted story kept the central plot idea of a lower-middle class lady who feels inferior at an elite social gathering. The student-made adaptations, on the other hand, showed considerable variations from the original in terms of the setting, characters and plot development. The same story by Virginia Woolf was adapted into a story about an Arab girl who feels insecure at a school prom held in the United States. The students used simple language and clearly mentioned that the protagonist felt insecure, rather than using descriptive sentences to hint at the main idea, as is shown in the student-generated sentence: “She was instantly consumed by feelings of insecurity and inferiority to her classmates when the popular students started looking at her and making fun of how she looked.”

### Linguistic and stylistic features of non-native texts

In order to address the second research question exploring the linguistic and stylistic features that are indicative of a non-native writer with an Arabic linguistic background, the AI- and student-generated texts were input into ChatGPT-4 with a prompt asking the model to determine whether each text was written by a native or non-native writer giving reasons and examples. The model identified all the student-generated stories as non-native texts while labeling 14 out of the 15 AI-generated stories as native. The reasons and examples provided by the AI model to explain why a text was likely written by a native or non-native writer were analyzed using a coding scheme based on common categorization patterns. These patterns primarily include sentence structure, word choice, and cultural context.

#### Sentence structure

The AI model described the ChatGPT-generated texts as having complex sentence structure and natural flow. The examples showing complexity and smooth flow included five examples with an adverbial phrase starting with the conjunction ‘as’ like in “as weeks pass, as Jamie grew older, as she circles the room, as Marla falls to the ground, and as the sea crashes.” Other examples of naturally flowing sentences included those with adverbial phrases describing the circumstances surrounding an action, especially ones having the preposition ‘with’ as in ‘with a sense of pity and fear, with a sense of nostalgia, and with a heavy heart.’ A word frequency count of these two discourse markers ‘with’ and ‘as’ made by the corpus linguistic tool AntConc revealed that ‘with’ was used 139 times in the AI-generated texts compared to 74 times in students’ texts, while ‘as’ was used 124 times in the AI texts compared to 87 times in students’ texts. The word ‘with’ was used in adverbial phrases describing action or circumstances in 33% of its occurrences in the AI texts compared to 15% in the students’ texts, while the word ‘as’ was used in adverbial clauses in 80% of the word occurrences in the AI texts compared to 3.5% in students’ texts. This suggests that the use of such adverbial phrases adds to the complexity of sentence structures characterizing native discourse, and that non-native writers are seldom aware of their use and function in discourse.

The AI model, ChatGPT-4, described the sentence structure of students’ texts as repetitive, long, awkward, and overly simple. Examples of repetitive structures included ‘he doesn’t do any action concerning this, she was born in Egypt and lived there, she fixed it and put it together again, and a poor home in which a poor family consisting of 3 lives’. The tendency of Arab writers to use repetitive synonyms and ideas in writing English texts is attributed to the frequent use of repetition in Arabic as a form of emphasis. The same finding was highlighted in previous research, which noted that Arab writers’ English style tends to rely on repetition and exaggeration rather than introducing new ideas to persuade their readers (Feghali, [1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR14 "Feghali, E. (1997). Arab cultural communication patterns. International Journal of Intercultural Relations, 21(3), 345–378.")). Examples of long sentences included sentences that had more than 3 clauses, mostly linked by the coordinating conjunctions ‘and’ and ‘so’. Table [1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#Tab1) shows the high frequency of the two conjunctions in students’ texts, which is attributed to L1 influence, since Arabic is a conjunction-dense language where conjunctions often replace punctuation marks in English (Farghal, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR13 "Farghal, M. (2017). Textual Issues Relating to Cohesion and Coherence in Arabic/English Translation. Jordan Journal of Modern Languages and Literature, 9(1), 29–50.")). Examples of awkward structures mentioned by ChatGPT included ‘her father was a worker who wasted his salary’, ‘he thought of his father’s friend to help him’, and ‘after years of him neglecting his mother’. The awkwardness of these expressions stems from their relation to Arabic as L1, which tends to overuse clitic presumptive pronouns that resume reference to nouns previously mentioned (Farghal, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR13 "Farghal, M. (2017). Textual Issues Relating to Cohesion and Coherence in Arabic/English Translation. Jordan Journal of Modern Languages and Literature, 9(1), 29–50.")). As for the simple structures highlighted by ChatGPT, they can be attributed to the students’ lack of complex vocabulary, which leads them to use simple structures, basic verbs like ‘think, tell, give, take, go’, and basic adjectives like ‘good, bad, happy and beautiful’ (see Table[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#Tab2)). In contrast, the AI-generated texts replaced these basic forms with more complex ones like the verbs ‘haunt, offer, blend, unfold, insist, drift’ and the adjectives ‘charming, thrilling, uneasy, disdained, and fascinated’.

#### Word choice

In terms of word choice, the ChatGPT model highlighted a number of differences between the AI- and student-generated texts. The model mentioned that the AI-generated texts had complex vocabulary and described vivid imagery using ‘show-not-tell’ expressions. Examples of complex and imagery-rich vocabulary from the AI text included ‘heart full of love’, ‘once seemed like a wave of despair’, ‘gilded cage’, ‘thrill’, ‘unease’, ‘howling’, ‘chilling’, ‘weathered’, ‘feeling the power of her ancestors’, ‘fearing the loss of her husband’, and ‘flaunting her extravagant lifestyle’. It is worth mentioning that many of the advanced vocabulary items depicted by the language model as complicated and refined included the use of participial phrases composed of a present participle form (a verb ending in –ing) to describe an ongoing action or the state of the subject as in “She wraps her hand around the talisman, feeling the power of her ancestors flow through her.” The AntConc tool was utilized to investigate the use of participle forms in each text type, and the results showed that the AI texts had 450 instances compared to 306 in the students’ texts.

On the other hand, the students’ word choice was described by the model as overly simplistic, exaggerated, awkward and redundant. The examples mentioned included ‘was there for her’ and ‘to be on the safe side’ (overly simple), ‘very desperate’ and ‘extremely happy’ (exaggerated), ‘blood analysis’, ‘Eden’s Heaven’ and ‘silence hits like a thunderstorm’ (awkward) and ‘evil and greedy’, ‘used to abuse’, and ‘fed up and mad’ (redundant). The frequencies of the intensifiers ‘very’ and ‘extremely’ were analyzed by the AntConc corpus tool, and the results showed that these intensifiers were only used in the students’ texts (24 times) but did not occur in any of the AI texts, which shows the students’ tendency to use exaggerated language forms. The simplicity of the language forms that the students used is also reflected in the type-token ratio values given in Table[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#Tab3), which shows that the students’ texts had lower lexical density manifested in their use of simple redundant words. The awkward word choices highlighted by the model are mainly attributed to the literal translation of these expressions from Arabic as in ‘blood analysis’ and ‘Eden’s Heaven’. Hence, the characterization of the students’ writing as overly simplistic, exaggerated, and redundant aligns with previous research findings that associated Arabic writers’ English style with repetition, indirectness and exaggeration (Feghali, [1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR14 "Feghali, E. On the other hand, _The Great Gatsby,_ was in fact a story written by Scott Fitzgerald, but it did not include the three expressions mentioned by ChatGPT: ‘city man, gold rings and gilded cage.’ However, the story text included more than thirty examples of the advanced vocabulary used in the AI-generated texts and highlighted by ChatGPT as characteristic of native language style. These included words like ‘façade, unease, gruff, disdain, unfold, solemn, extravagant and weighed down’. This implies the model’s reliance on data from classic short stories to make up the story adaptations under study.

As for the cultural implications in the non-native texts written by the students, the model highlighted husband-wife traditional roles in one story and traditionally arranged marriages and social expectations in another as indicative of non-native Eastern cultures. The husband-wife roles in the students’ text depicted the woman as responsible for household chores, while the man was portrayed as the primary bread-winner in the family, a stereotype that was not portrayed in any of the AI-generated texts. It is worth mentioning that although ChatGPT identified all students’ texts as written by non-native writers, it was not able to detect that the writer had an Arab background in all the stories that were set in western contexts. It identified the background as Arab only in the stories that included Arabic names of persons and places, and the model actually mentioned Arabic names as a determiner of the writer’s linguistic background in these stories.

The above analysis of the non-native features in the students’ texts, compared to the AI-generated texts, highlights characteristics likely to define English discourse produced by Arab writers. Arab writers tend to write English sentences that have long, repetitive structures with excessive use of coordinating conjunctions, make use of basic language forms, contain frequent intensifiers, and reflect socio-cultural ideologies. Additionally, non-native English discourse produced by Arab writers includes high frequencies of basic verbs like ‘take, give, go, tell, think, good, happy and beautiful’, and lacks complex sentence structures, particularly those containing adverbial, adjectival and participial phrases used to describe actions, processes and participants.

### Pedagogical implications

In response to the third research question, several pedagogical implications can be drawn from the stylometric comparison between ChatGPT-generated and student-generated texts. First, the study provides a framework for analyzing a literary text in terms of a set of stylistic and linguistic features that can attribute it to a human author or an AI program. Since human readers cannot easily distinguish AI-generated from human-generated language (Clark et al., [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR9 "Clark, E. et al. (2021). All that’s `human’ is not gold: Evaluating human evaluation of generated text. In Proceedings of the 59th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics and the 11th International Joint Conference on Natural Language Processing, 1. 7282–7296. 
                  https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.acl-long.565
                  
                "); Kreps et al., [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR25 "Kreps, S., McCain, R. M., & Brundage, M. (2022). All the news that’s fit to fabricate: AI-generated text as a tool of media misinformation. Journal of Experimental Political Science, 9(1), 104–117. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1017/XPS.2020.37
                  
                ")), it is important to inform readers, especially educators, of ways in which they can detect AI features in a text. The teachers can use the proposed framework to analyze the word frequencies of adjectives, adjectival modifiers and fronted adverbial and adjectival phrases as possible determiners of AI features. Second, the students can also benefit from studying ways in which ChatGPT creates coherence and produces text that is analytic and descriptive. For example, they can try adding more adjectives and adjectival modifiers to the nouns they have used in a text. They can also practice fronting adverbial and adjectival phrases to give their text a refined polish and make it vocabulary-rich. Third, the students should use the AI technology as a facilitator rather than a main source of information, considering the shortcomings it has in terms of depth, authenticity and referencing. They can also use ChatGPT to edit their own work and find out the language and style errors that the model will correct.

Teachers sometimes correct students’ work and grade it without informing the students of how to correct each error. Non-native students are believed to make repetitive errors due to a mismatch between students’ and teachers’ perceptions of what constitutes a language error (Nguyen and Luu, [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR34 "Nguyen, N. X., & Luu, N. (2021). EFL learners’ perceptions of teachers’ corrective feedback for pronunciation. International Journal of Science and Management Studies, 4, 266–281. 
                  https://doi.org/10.51386/25815946/ijsms-v4i4p125
                  
                "); Emara & Shaker, [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR12 "Emara, I. F., & Shaker, N. H. (2024). The impact of non-native English speakers’ phonological and prosodic features on automatic speech recognition accuracy. Speech Communication, 157, Article 103038. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2024.103038
                  
                ")). Also, writing instructors in Arab countries tend to correct students’ grammar and punctuation rather than content, style and organization of ideas (Abu Rass, [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR3 "Abu Rass, R. (2015). Challenges face Arab students in writing well-developed paragraphs in English. English Language Teaching, 8(10), 49–59. 
                  https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v8n10p49
                  
                ")). ChatGPT, on the other hand, can provide a more informative and less intimidating environment in which the students can observe how the model corrects their language and refines their writing style. It is also beneficial in highlighting the awkward writing styles which make a student’s text non-native like, a practice that is often overlooked by teachers. The model is able to identify the non-native features in a student’s text as it compares the text to the huge native data set it is trained with, a practice which cannot easily be done by a non-native English instructor.

Though several ethical concerns are raised with the increasing use of ChatGPT in educational settings, it is inevitable to incorporate it in the language learning context. Students with different linguistic backgrounds prefer the use of GPT bots which they regard as affordable, easy to use and highly competent (Liu et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR27 "Liu, G. Computer Assisted Language Learning. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2024.2310288
                  
                ")). ESL students were found to prosper in a digital learning environment which instilled in them positive emotions of engagement and interest and reduced the negative emotions of anxiety and boredom (Sadan et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR38 "Sadan, M., Lipka, O., & Katzir, T. Yet, it is important for teachers to guide students into how to use digital learning tools like ChatGPT to achieve learning objectives while mitigating the possible negative consequences like plagiarism and dissemination of false information (Tlili et al., [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR44 "Tlili, A., et al. ### Limitations and suggestions for future research

One of the limitations of the present study is the relatively small dataset used: 30 adapted short stories, 15 student-generated and 15 AI-generated. However, despite the limited dataset, the author was able to use concordances and automated word analysis tools to identify common linguistic and stylistic patterns in both student- and AI-generated texts. The collected texts can be used as a preliminary dataset which may be expanded in future research to produce more generalizable findings. Another limitation is the use of one AI generative language model (ChatGPT-4) to generate adapted stories and identify non-native features of student-generated texts. The model was used on basis of the students’ familiarity with it and tendency to use it in academic contexts; yet, future research could explore the use of other AI language models in identifying non-native stylometric features. A third limitation is the gender distribution of the students, as the majority were females (73 out of the 75 students who wrote the story adaptations). This may have influenced the students’ writing style, especially in relation to the use of intensifiers and expressions of uncertainty (see Table[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#Tab2)). A previous study showed that Egyptian female writers tended to use more intensifiers and uncertainty markers in their English discourse (Emara, [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR11 "Emara, I. (2017). Gender identity construction in Facebook statuses of Egyptian young adults. Cairo Studies in English, 1, 86–111.")). Therefore, it is recommended that future research examine the stylometric features of non-native discourse produced by both male and female writers. Future research could also explore gender-based linguistic differences by using AI language models to detect stylistic variations between male and female writers.

## Conclusion and further implications

The linguistic and stylistic comparison between student-generated and ChatGPT-generated short story adaptations provided herein provides an analytical framework that can be used to detect AI-specific language and style features. The qualitative analysis of theme, character, plot and word choice in students’ versus AI’s stories showed the students’ tendency to develop the original theme, deviate from the main plot line, create different characters, adapt the context to their cultural backgrounds and use simple language with word repetitions. The AI model, on the other hand, remained loyal to the theme, plot, and character description, but tended to use language that was more analytic, descriptive, complex and free from cultural bias. The implications of the study can be used to inform teachers and educators about the possible ways of detecting AI-specific features to avoid plagiarism and help refine the students’ language and writing style in the ESL context.

As for the cultural implications, the students tended to reflect some of the Arab cultural values like the subordination of women to their husbands, the unlikeliness of portraying an Arab wife as a cheater and the removal of scenes with alcohol drinking, whereas ChatGPT avoided all racial remarks and religion-related expressions. The AI model also made several references to technology and the digital world, stressing the importance of modern developments against the old ways of thinking. The quantitative analysis investigated the stylometric features proposed by Holmes ([1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR20 "Holmes, D. I. (1994). Authorship attribution. Computers and the Humanities, 28, 87–106. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01830689
                  
                ")) and adopted in various recent studies, namely word and sentence length as well as function and lexical word frequencies. The results showed that the word and sentence count did not significantly differ, neither did the frequencies of nouns, verbs, adverbs and function words (such as pronouns and modals). The features that showed significant differences were mainly the number of adjectives, adjectival modifiers, fronted adverbial and adjectival phrases, participial phrases, the readability index and type-token ration (lexical density), all of which showed higher values in the AI-generated text. The students’ texts were characterized by long repetitive structures, excessive use of coordinating conjunctions, frequent use of basic content words and simple sentences. The results support previous work which found AI-generated texts to be more analytic, descriptive and less readable than human-generated text (Georgiou, [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR16 "Georgiou, G. (2024). Differentiating between human-written and AI-generated texts using linguistic features automatically extracted from an online computational tool. Arxi. 
                  https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2407.03646
                  
                "); Markowitz et al., [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR31 "Markowitz, D. M., Hancock, J. T., & Bailenson, J. N. (2024). Linguistic markers of inherently false AI communication and intentionally false human communication: Evidence from hotel reviews. Journal of Language and Social Psychology, 43(1), 63–82. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X231200201
                  
                ")). While the students maintained cohesion through the use of discourse markers, the AI model created coherence by dividing the text into smaller paragraphs, with each paragraph centered on a specific idea or detail, a finding which was also made by Herbold et al. ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR19 "Herbold, S., et al. (2023). A large-scale comparison of human-written versus ChatGPT-generated essays. Scientific Reports, 13, 18617. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9
                  
                ")).

The present study has significant implications in the fields of pedagogy and native language identification. The results show how AI language models like ChatGPT-4 can be used to facilitate the learning process by highlighting the non-native-like language structures that the students need to work on to improve. Since educators often prioritize correcting grammar, spelling and punctuation errors over stylistic issues, AI models can offer valuable guidance to students to refine their writing style and render it more native-like. Yet, the use of AI models in education should be supervised by human instructors to review the information provided and ensure its accuracy and credibility.

A further implication the present research has is related to the use of AI language models in native language identification. Language models like ChatGPT and Gemini are able to identify whether a text is written by a native or non-native writer due to the mismatch between the non-native forms and the huge dataset of native forms that the models are trained with. However, the language models are not able to clearly detect the writer’s L1 based on the linguistic and stylistic patterns used. Previous studies utilized ChatGPT to identify a writer’s native language on basis of L1-induced language errors (Zhang & Salle, [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-025-00388-z#ref-CR46 "Zhang, W. & Salle, A. (2023). Native language identification with large language models. Arxiv. 
                  http://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.07819v1.pdf
                  
                ")). Yet, no previous studies attempted to detect a writer’s native language on the basis of stylometric features, especially in the context of Arabic as L1, hence the contribution of the present study. In the current study, the students’ texts were edited for grammar and spelling errors, so the non-native forms detected by the AI model were mainly stylistic deviations from native English discourse rather than L1-based errors. AI language models can be efficient in detecting non-native features, but human annotation is of great value here since humans can attribute the non-native features to the influence of L1. This has significant benefits in the pedagogical context where the students can use AI models to identify their non-native stylistic problems and edit their writing using the alternative native-like forms provided by the language models. The use of AI models to detect non-native features in discourse can also contribute to forensic linguistics, where the identification of an author’s or a speaker’s linguistic background may serve as a key piece of evidence.
